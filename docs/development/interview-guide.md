## 9. Ten hard interviewer questions, with strong answers

**1. Your evaluation is on synthetic data you generated. Isn't that circular?** Partly, and I say so on the About page. Circularity would be fatal if the simulator and the detector shared assumptions — but they don't overlap in the way that would matter. The simulator is a forward causal model: queueing delay, timeout logistics, retry amplification, memory accumulation. The detector is a backward statistical model that knows nothing about queueing theory; it sees six numbers per service and has to recover the cause. Nothing about `1/(1-u)` is encoded in the detector. So the honest claim is narrow: on telemetry with realistic seasonality, autocorrelation, heavy latency tails and graph-mediated propagation, this pipeline recovers injected root causes at the measured rate. That's an upper bound on real-world performance, because real telemetry has failure modes I didn't model — multi-tenancy, clock skew, partial metric loss, concurrent independent incidents. What the synthetic setup genuinely buys me is a labelled test set at all, which is the thing nobody has for real incidents. In production I'd bootstrap labels from confirmed post-mortems and keep the synthetic benchmark as a regression gate in CI.

**2. Why not just give the LLM all the telemetry and ask it?** I tried a version of that and it fails in three specific ways. First, calibration: I need a probability I can threshold and a margin over the runner-up, and LLM verbalized confidence isn't calibrated in any way I can measure — my logistic regression has a calibration table on the Evaluation page. Second, cost and latency: the pipeline runs in single-digit milliseconds and can score every tick for every service continuously; you cannot afford an LLM call per tick per service. Third, evaluability: when the classifier is wrong I can inspect coefficients, find that `deploy_recent` was over-weighted, and fix it. When an LLM is wrong I can rewrite the prompt and hope. The division I settled on is that the LLM does what it's genuinely better at than any model I could train on this data volume — reading heterogeneous evidence, noticing that a claim doesn't follow, and writing an explanation an on-call engineer can act on at 3 a.m. It's also allowed to disagree, and the UI shows the disagreement rather than hiding it.

**3. Walk me through why you need AR(1) whitening. Isn't a z-score enough?** No, and this was the bug that took the longest. A plain z-score assumes independent observations. Metric residuals are strongly autocorrelated — around ρ = 0.85 at fifteen-second granularity — because CPU and latency at t are physically dependent on t−1. If you compute the standard deviation of an autocorrelated series and then threshold single observations against it, your effective sample size is far smaller than your nominal one, the variance of the sample statistic is understated, and you get long runs of consecutive "anomalies" during ordinary drift. Whitening with `innovation = r_t − ρ·r_{t−1}` gives approximately independent residuals, so percentile thresholds mean roughly what they claim to. I also use MAD rather than standard deviation for scale, because if the fitting window contains any real incidents the standard deviation absorbs them and the detector desensitizes itself to exactly the events it should catch. And I still keep the k-of-n persistence gate on top — whitening reduces the false-positive rate but doesn't eliminate it, and requiring three of five consecutive breaches converts a per-tick error rate into a much lower per-incident one at the cost of a bounded detection delay, which the benchmark reports.

**4. Six services are breaching simultaneously. How do you know which one is the cause?** Three signals, and the interesting one is the third. Magnitude alone is actively misleading, because the edge service aggregates every downstream problem and is usually the loudest victim. Onset ordering helps: I run two-sided CUSUM per service to estimate a changepoint, and causes tend to move first — but at fifteen-second granularity in a fast cascade, onsets can be within one or two ticks and the ordering gets noisy. The signal that actually carries the load is upstreamness: for each anomalous service, what fraction of the *other* anomalous services transitively depend on it? A slow database is depended upon by every other breaching service; a breaching gateway is depended upon by none of them. That's a structural argument that doesn't degrade when timing is ambiguous. I weight magnitude 0.40, onset lead 0.24, upstreamness 0.24, deploy proximity 0.12, and the per-scenario localization table shows where it's weakest — cascade scenarios are the hardest, because the origin and its immediate victims are genuinely close in both time and blame.

**5. Those weights are hand-tuned. Isn't that a hack?** It's a prior, and it's the honest choice at my data volume — but you're right that it isn't learned, so let me be precise about what I'd do differently with more data. The blame function is a linear scorer over four features with weights I chose from domain reasoning and then sanity-checked against per-scenario localization accuracy. With enough labelled incidents I'd replace it with a learn-to-rank model — LambdaMART over the same features plus more, trained on (incident, candidate service) pairs with the true root as the positive. I didn't do that here because with roughly eighty episodes and nine candidate services I'd be fitting a ranker on a few hundred pairs with heavy structure, and I'd rather ship a transparent prior I can defend than an overfit ranker with a better number. What I did do is avoid tuning the weights against the test metric iteratively, which would have quietly turned my benchmark into a training set.

**6. Your RCA classifier gets high accuracy on seven classes. Is it leaking?** It was, and finding that is the part of the project I'd most want to talk about. My first feature set one-hot encoded service identity. But each scenario has a fixed root service, so `service == postgres-primary` is nearly a perfect predictor of `db_latency_saturation` — the model wasn't diagnosing anything, it was memorising my scenario table. Two fixes. I generalised service identity to service *kind* — edge, app, datastore, cache, external — which is a genuine causal prior (datastores saturate, externals go down, apps leak memory) and doesn't identify the scenario. And I switched cross-validation to GroupKFold grouped on episode seed, so no test episode shares a random seed with any training episode. Accuracy dropped, and the number on the Evaluation page is the post-fix number. There's residual leakage risk I'll name: the log-pattern-rate features are informative partly because my log synthesizer emits `lock_wait` under datastore latency, which is realistic but is still my own generative choice. That's why I report the rule-based baseline alongside the learned model — if the learned model only barely beats a transparent rule scorer, the features are doing more work than the learning is, and that's worth knowing.

**7. Why lexical retrieval instead of embeddings? That looks like you avoided the harder thing.** It's a measured trade-off, and the measurement is published. My corpus is fourteen documents, about sixty chunks, dense with exact technical tokens — `pg_terminate_backend`, `HikariCP`, `maxmemory`, `5xx`. BM25 is strong exactly there, and vocabulary mismatch, which is where dense retrieval wins decisively, is limited because both the queries and the documents come from the same technical register. I did add a latent-semantic arm via TF-IDF plus truncated SVD and fused the two with reciprocal rank fusion, so paraphrase is partly handled. In exchange I get zero model downloads, deterministic results, sub-millisecond retrieval, and a demo that runs on any laptop with no GPU. The Evaluation page reports recall\@3, MRR and nDCG\@3 against gold documents, so if the number were poor you'd see it. Two caveats I'd raise unprompted: this doesn't scale — at a few thousand runbooks, lexical-only retrieval degrades and my BM25 implementation is an O(N·|q|) loop I'd have to replace anyway. And I report retrieval twice, once with an oracle class query to isolate retriever quality and once with the predicted class to capture the end-to-end number, because query formulation depends on the RCA prediction and reporting only the oracle number would flatter the system.

**8. What happens when your system recommends the wrong fix?** This was the design constraint I cared most about, because a system that can't be wrong isn't a system. Three layers. First, the action space is closed: actions come only from the `actions` manifest of retrieved runbooks, so it can't invent `DROP TABLE`. Second, the policy engine gates on risk, reversibility, blast radius and severity — anything medium-risk or above, anything irreversible, anything affecting all users, and anything on a SEV1 requires human approval, which is a real block on an asyncio event, not a UI decoration. Third, and most important, remediation is followed by independent verification: the detector — which had no part in choosing the action — re-evaluates and requires six consecutive ticks below threshold. Because remediation mutates real fault objects in the environment, a wrong action genuinely fails to fix anything, verification fails, and the incident escalates to a human with the hypothesis marked invalidated. I demo that path deliberately. In production I'd add automatic rollback of the attempted action on non-recovery, and a blast-radius circuit breaker that stops the system from attempting a second action after a failed first one.

**9. How does this scale from nine services to five thousand?** Different parts break at different points, so let me take them in order. Detection scales fine and is the easy part: it's per-service and stateless given fitted parameters, so it shards trivially — a Flink or Faust job keyed by service, with parameters refit nightly from a feature store. Roughly a millisecond per service per tick means one core handles a few thousand services at fifteen-second granularity. Localization is where the design actually strains: my blame function iterates over all firing services and computes transitive dependents, which is fine at nine and painful when a large cascade fires eight hundred services. I'd restrict the candidate set to a k-hop neighbourhood of the firing set, precompute reachability, and switch from a scorer to a proper causal-graph method — something in the PageRank-on-the-reversed-dependency-graph family, which is what production systems converge on. RCA classification is per-incident, not per-service, so it doesn't care. Retrieval needs the pgvector-plus-embeddings swap I mentioned. And the genuinely hard problem at that scale isn't compute, it's that single-fault assumption: with five thousand services you always have several unrelated incidents in flight, so incident *grouping* — clustering firing services into distinct causal incidents before diagnosing any of them — becomes a prerequisite step I haven't built.

**10. If you had two more weeks, what would you build?** Three things, in priority order. First, counterfactual validation of the root cause: right now the classifier asserts a cause and verification only checks recovery after the fact. Because I have a simulator, I could run an intervention — clear the hypothesised fault in a forked copy of the world, roll forward, and check whether the symptoms actually resolve. That turns "the model ranked this highest" into "removing this cause demonstrably fixes it," which is a much stronger claim and is genuinely a causal-inference argument rather than a correlational one. Second, incident grouping, for the multi-fault reason in the previous answer — it's the biggest gap between this and something deployable. Third, human feedback as a training signal: every approval, rejection and alternative-selection is a label, and I already log them, so I'd close the loop by retraining the ranker on operator decisions with the usual care about feedback loops and selection bias. If you asked me what I'd *cut*, I'd say the impact model's dollar figures — they're the least defensible part of the product, since the computation is real but the per-request revenue constants are assumptions, and I'd rather report error-budget burn and affected-request counts, which are measurable, and leave the money conversion to whoever actually knows their unit economics.
